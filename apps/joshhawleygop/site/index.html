01

The carpetbagger from Vienna, Virginia

In January 2019, weeks after being sworn in, Hawley bought a five-bedroom house in Vienna, Virginia, a Washington suburb. It is now valued at around $1.7 million. That spring he sold his home in Columbia, Missouri.

So what did Missouri's "man of the people" list as his Missouri home? His sister's house in Ozark. The Kansas City Star reported in 2020 that Hawley was registered to vote at his sister's place, and used it for his Missouri Bar registration too, while he and his family lived full time in Virginia. When the Hawley family was home during a protest in January 2021, the house they were in was the one in Vienna.

Only after years of getting caught did he bother building a Missouri paper trail. A house in Christian County that his parents bought land for, with Hawley on the deed, was finished in 2021. In late 2023, heading into a re-election year, he bought a second house nearby.

Try registering to vote at your sister's house while you live in a mansion two states away. See how that goes for you. For Josh Hawley, it went fine for years.

His defense: he now owns property in Christian County, one house finished in 2021 and another bought in late 2023, just in time for re-election, and he filed from a Missouri address. The law only requires senators to be "inhabitants" when elected. Legal? Sure. A Missourian? You decide.

02

He cheered the mob. Then he ran like hell.

Hawley was the first senator to say he would object to certifying the 2020 election, and on January 6, 2021 he did it. He objected to Pennsylvania's electoral votes, and the objection failed 92 to 7.

That morning, walking into the Capitol, he raised a fist toward the crowd gathered outside. According to the House January 6 committee, a Capitol Police officer said the gesture "riled up the crowd." Hours later, security footage showed Hawley running down a Capitol hallway and hurrying down a staircase to get away from the people he'd saluted.

Then, because shame is apparently not in his vocabulary, his campaign sold coffee mugs with the fist-pump picture on them. When Politico, whose photographer took the photo, objected, his lawyer said the campaign would not "engage in self-censorship."

"No one other than President Donald Trump himself is more responsible for Wednesday's coup attempt at the U.S. Capitol than one Joshua David Hawley." The Kansas City Star Editorial Board, January 2021

03

He called Medicaid cuts "morally wrong," then sold you out anyway

About 1.25 million Missourians rely on Medicaid and CHIP, and rural hospitals depend on it to keep the doors open. Hawley knew that. He said so himself, in public, more than once.

  1. May 2025Writes in the New York Times: "We must ignore calls to cut Medicaid." He calls benefit cuts "both morally wrong and politically suicidal."
  2. June 28, 2025Warns that Republicans "cannot take away health care from working people."
  3. July 1, 2025Votes for the "One Big Beautiful Bill," which includes hundreds of billions of dollars in Medicaid cuts. The Missouri Hospital Association warned that its provider-tax cap alone would cost the state hundreds of millions.
  4. July 15, 2025Introduces a bill to undo some of the Medicaid cuts he had voted for two weeks earlier.

His excuse is a $50 billion rural hospital fund he says he got in exchange. Translation: he knew exactly what the cuts would do to Missouri, took the deal anyway, then filed a bill to undo them so he could pretend he was on your side. If they were bad enough to repeal two weeks later, they were bad enough to vote no on. And his vote mattered: the bill passed 51–50, with the Vice President breaking the tie. Hawley could have stopped it. He didn't.

04

He played politics with sick veterans

The PACT Act extended health care to veterans made sick by toxic burn pits. Hawley voted for it in June 2022. A month later, with the bill nearly finished, he switched to no, part of a Republican bloc that stalled it. Veterans camped on the Capitol steps in protest. Only when the cameras and the outrage got too hot did Hawley suddenly rediscover his conscience and flip back to yes in August.

Then in 2024, the man who ran from the Capitol accused his opponent, Marine veteran Lucas Kunce, of "stolen valor" over his Border Patrol tactical training. A Border Patrol spokesperson confirmed the training was real. Veterans from both parties condemned the attack.

Credit where due: in February 2025 he was the only Republican to vote for a resolution protecting burn-pit veterans' benefits. One vote. It doesn't erase the rest.

05

You voted. He doesn't care.

In November 2024, Missourians passed Amendment 3 and put reproductive freedom in the state constitution, with 51.7% of the vote. Hawley opposed it.

Now he's campaigning for the 2026 Amendment 3 on the November 3, 2026 ballot. It would repeal most of what voters just approved and replace it with a near-total ban, with rape and incest exceptions only up to 12 weeks. "I absolutely am going to be involved," he told reporters. In September 2026 he took it national, launching a "Declaration for Life" that calls for banning mifepristone, the abortion pill, and urging Republican candidates to sign on.

Missouri voters made their decision in 2024. Hawley thinks he knows better than you, so he's going to keep forcing the question until he gets the answer he wants. That's not democracy. That's a tantrum.

06

The ladder climber who ran against ladder climbers

In 2016, running for attorney general, Hawley ran a TV ad where he walked through a maze of ladders and attacked "career politicians just climbing the ladder, using one office to get another."

He took office as attorney general in January 2017. Ten months later he was already climbing the next ladder, announcing a run for the U.S. Senate. You can't make this up. A state audit later found that political consultants who went on to run his Senate campaign had been directing taxpayer-paid staff in the attorney general's office, starting the month he took office.

His defense: fellow Republican Secretary of State Jay Ashcroft closed the case in 2019, saying he found no evidence Hawley broke election law, and Hawley insists there was "no campaign" until October 2017. The campaign consultants running meetings in the AG's office that whole time might beg to differ.

Smears first, cries "censorship" later

During Judge Ketanji Brown Jackson's Supreme Court confirmation, Hawley claimed she had a "pattern" of going easy on child pornography offenders. PolitiFact found he had cherry-picked the record. Her sentences were in line with national practice, and Hawley had himself voted to confirm Trump judges who handed down similar sentences.

And when Simon & Schuster dropped his book deal after January 6, America's toughest man whined that it was an attack on the First Amendment. It wasn't: the First Amendment restricts the government, not publishers. He published the book elsewhere anyway. (A later book was titled Manhood. Written by the guy in the running video.) Promoting his big-tech book at a Washington Post event, he told the room, "Don't try to censor, cancel and silence me here." The moderator answered, "Senator, we're hosting you here."
